Why you need a key

Any program that asks Google's Gemini a question needs a key: your own code, an app, or Spark, the study buddy on this site. So why a key at all? Let's try to understand.

Say you join a library. The books are there for everyone. But to borrow one, you show your own card. The card says who is borrowing, and how many books that person may take.

Gemini works the same way. Each question to it uses a little of Google's computing. Your key tells Google the question is yours. Google also sets how much each key may ask, per minute and per day. Those limits change, so check them in AI Studio.

That card is what we call an API key. First, get your key. Then, if you are learning with us, give it to Spark.

Get your Gemini API key

Three steps, all in Google AI Studio.

1

Create the key

Open Google AI Studio → API keys and sign in with your Google account. Click Create API key.

Give your key a name you will recognise, like Learn AI by Building. Keep the Default Gemini Project, or create a project of your own, as here. Then click Create key.

2

Copy the key

The key appears in API key details. Click Copy key, or the copy icon beside it. Now paste it wherever you need it.

3

Delete a key, when you are done with it

Come back here when you stop using a key, or when someone else may have seen it. On the API keys page, open the ⋮ menu on the key's row. Click Delete key, then Delete.

Deleting cannot be undone. Anything using that key stops working. To carry on, make a new key, and use the new one instead.

AI Studio will not let you create a key? Check that you are 18 or older, that your Google account has your age verified, and that you are in a country where it works.

Keep your key safe

Your key is like a password. Whoever has it can ask Gemini as you, and use up your limits.

  • Never share it. Not in a chat, an email, a screenshot or a comment.
  • Only paste it where you trust the page. On this site, that is Spark, and nowhere else.
  • If it may have leaked, delete it (step 3, above), and make a new one.

How programs use a key safely, and why it never goes into shared code, comes later in the series.

That is your key. If you came for the key alone, you are done. To use it on this site, read on.

Use your key with Spark

Every lesson on this site has Spark, our study buddy. Spark thinks with Gemini, using your own key. You give it the key once; it stays in this browser, and goes only to Google, together with your question.

1

Open Spark

Open any lesson, for example Chapter 0. Press the round ✦ Spark button at the bottom right.

2

Paste your key

Paste the key into Your Gemini API key. It shows only as dots. Press Save key & start. (If you came in through ⚙ settings, the button says Save settings.)

Chrome may offer "Save password?" when you paste it. Choose Never. The key is not a password for this site.

3

Ask a question

Type something into Ask about this unit and press the arrow. Start simple: Hello Spark.

4

See the answer

Spark answers from the page you are on. Then ask it something real, like Summarise this page in three sentences.

On a shared computer, clear the key before you leave. Open Spark's ⚙ settings, empty the key box, and press Save settings.

When Spark goes wrong

Read the message first. Spark tells you what Google said.

  • "That API key was rejected by Google — let's re-enter it." Part of the key was missed, or a space came along, or the key was deleted. Copy it again with Copy key, and paste it again.
  • "The quota for your key is used up for now — wait a minute and try again." You asked more than your key may ask in a minute. Wait, and ask again. AI Studio's Rate Limit page shows your limits.
  • A message from Google about your location or region. The Gemini API does not work in your country yet. The list of countries is in the references.
  • Spark says nothing at all. Check your internet connection, reload the page, and try once more.

Still stuck? Use Gemini chat at gemini.google.com for now, and come back to Spark later.

Your homework

Ask, then check

Open Chapter 0, connect Spark, and ask it one question about the page. Then check its answer against the page itself. Was it right?
