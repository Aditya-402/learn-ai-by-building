Software already runs everything

Think about it. Your phone, your car, your bank. Behind each of them sit millions of lines of code. A person wrote every rule in them, and the machine follows those rules.

So a student asked me a fair question: if code can already run all of this, why do we need AI at all?

Let's try to understand. And let's start with a small puzzle. Here are two jobs for a computer.

Job A

Take a student's marks, and give the grade.

Job B

Take a photo, and say whether your friend is in it.

Before you read on, decide. For which job can you write down the exact rules? Hold on to your answer.

Job A: you write the rules

Say your college gives grades like this. Ninety and above is an A. Seventy five and above is a B. Below that, a C.

These are rules. You can write every one of them down. So the program is easy. Press ▶ Run, then change 82 to 95, and then to 60.

Same marks in, same grade out. Every single time. That is the strength of this kind of code. It is exact. And if someone asks why they got a B, you can show them the line.

A person writes the rules, and the computer follows them. This is what we call traditional programming.

Job B: the wall

Now job B. Say you are scrolling through the group photos from a college trip. In one second, you spot your friend. From the side. In a cap. Even in a dark, blurry one.

You find him in each of these in a second. Now write down how.

Let's try. Two eyes, a nose and a mouth? Everyone in the photo has that. Short hair? So do half the boys in your class. A beard? He shaved last week. Glasses? He took them off for the photo.

And it gets worse. Keep all four rules anyway. Then his cousin shows up in a photo. Same hair, same beard, same glasses. Your program says, that is your friend.

Every rule you add to tell them apart breaks on some other photo.
The wall
You know your friend's face perfectly. But you cannot write down how you know it. And if you cannot write the rules, you cannot write the program.

Faces are not special. Understanding what you say to your phone, reading messy handwriting, spotting a spam message. Same wall, every time.

And this really happened. In 1979, a company called DEC built a program to check its customers' computer orders. It started with 250 hand-written rules. By 1983, it had about 3,300. Its own builders wrote: "It is difficult now to believe R1 will ever be done."

How you really learned it

So let me ask you a different question. How did you learn your friend's face in the first place?

Nobody gave you rules. You simply saw him many times. In class, in the canteen, on the trip. Each time, you knew: this is him. And slowly, your brain worked out the pattern on its own.

No rules. Many examples.

So here is the idea. What if we do the same for a computer? We don't give it rules. We give it thousands of photos, each with the answer. This is your friend. This is not. And the computer works out the rules by itself.

Learning the rules from examples, instead of being given them, is what we call machine learning. Most of what we call AI today is built on this idea.

The idea is older than you might think. In 1959, a researcher at IBM wrote a program to play checkers, a board game a bit like a simpler chess. It learned by playing. After eight to ten hours of practice, it played better than the person who wrote it.

Two ways to build it

Let's put the two side by side.

Traditional coding

rules → logic → program → answers

Machine learning

data → training → model → deployment

In traditional coding, a person discovers the rules. The rules become logic. The logic goes into a program. And the program gives you the answers.

Machine learning starts from the other end. You start with data. The data goes through a training algorithm. Out comes a model. And the model is put to work in a real app.

Finding the rules from data is what we call training. What comes out is what we call a model. Sometimes a model is a set of rules the computer found. Most often, it is just numbers. Either way, no human wrote them. And putting the model to work is what we call deployment.

Does it really work?

Let's look at one famous yearly challenge, called ImageNet. Every year, teams from around the world sent in their programs. Each program looks at a photo, and names what is in it. Not faces. Things. Dogs, cars, mushrooms, a thousand kinds of things. It gets five guesses.

Wrong out of every 100 photos. Sources at the end of the page.

In 2010, the best program still got about 28 out of every 100 wrong. Then the models were improved, with better ways of learning. By 2015, the best one got fewer than 4 wrong. For comparison, one careful human tried the same challenge, and got about 5 wrong.

Nobody wrote the rules for a thousand kinds of things. The programs learned them. And today it is everywhere. In one large report this year, 88 out of every 100 organisations surveyed said they use AI.

Certain, or predicted?

But notice something. Even the best program still got some wrong. That is not a bug. That is how AI answers.

Traditional code is certain

Same input, same output, every single time.

AI predicts

It gives you its best guess, with how sure it is.

And you already live with predictions. Say you hear the weather report. Sixty percent chance of rain. You carry an umbrella, and sometimes it does not rain. Nobody calls the report broken. It told you how sure it was.

Or say Google Maps tells you, thirty five minutes. You reach in thirty four, or thirty six. If traffic catches up, it could take longer than that. That estimate is a prediction, and Google makes it with machine learning.

Predictions you already trust. So with AI, always ask one more question: how sure is it?
Careful here

Certain does not mean correct. If the rule is wrong, the code is wrong every single time.

So, AI for everything?

No. Go back to job A, the grades. Would you show a machine thousands of mark sheets, to learn a rule you can write in three lines?

It would get most of them right. But one day, it gives a B to someone with ninety five. And it cannot tell you why.

Because learning has a cost. It needs many examples. It is sometimes wrong. And it cannot always explain itself. Code with written rules has none of these costs.

So when you can write the rules, write them. Use learning only where you cannot. A well known engineering guide from Google puts it as rule number one: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning."

Fit for purpose thinking, not trend first thinking.

Most real systems use both

Say you pay a friend from your bank app. The money moving from your account follows exact rules. Not one rupee can go missing. That part is code.

But say a payment looks unusual. A big amount, at midnight, to a stranger. Your bank may stop it, or message you to check. Is this a fraud? Nobody can write all those rules. So that part is AI. It learns from past frauds, and it predicts.

In India, the Reserve Bank has even built an AI model to help banks catch accounts used for fraud. Same app. Code for the certain part. AI for the guess.

Key idea
It was never AI or coding. It is AI and coding, each for its own job.

The takeaway

So, why AI, when we already have traditional coding? AI did not come because traditional coding was bad. It came because some problems were never programming problems. They were learning problems.

When we know the rules, we program them. When we only have examples, we learn them.

But this opens a new question. If the machine learns from our examples, who picks the examples? And who checks what it learned?
